Simulated data. Ten simulated participants built to behave as the hypothesis states. These are not empirical results; the numbers show what the planned analysis returns when the truth is known.

A Second Look · work package 3 · validation of the planned analysis

Does the analysis measure what we set out to measure?

The complete planned analysis, run on data files in the exact layout the online task writes, from simulated participants whose true parameters are known.

Summary

The primary measure works. The simulated participants used disconfirming evidence 49% less under the direct mapping than under the rotated one. The analysis recovered a reduction of 44% (log difference put in −0.67, recovered −0.58). The contrast was significant at N = 10, t(9) = −2.47, p = .036, dz = −0.78, bootstrap p = .023.

The absolute level is still biased. Fitted wd came out at 0.43 and 0.77 where 0.47 and 0.92 had been put in (91% and 84%). The strength of the high evidence is measured rather than extrapolated, so the level no longer rests on an over-estimated psychometric slope. A test of wd against 1 still goes through an ideal-observer benchmark, below.

What follows for the real study is listed in the last section.

Method

Ten participants were simulated. Each judged which of two circles they controlled, under a direct (0°) and a rotated (90°) mapping in counterbalanced order, with control proportion set by a 1-up-2-down staircase that ran throughout. Per mapping there were 30 Task 1 trials (decision, then confidence rating on a 9-point scale) and 60 Task 2 trials (decision, a further 3 s of evidence, then the rating). The further evidence was of the same strength as the decision sample (low) or stronger (high), and always came from the true target. The high strength was not fixed: a second adaptive track (weighted up-down, 85% target) set it relative to the live staircase value, driven by strength trials: choice-only trials whose first look was already at the high strength, 20 per mapping in a short calibration block with feedback after the staircase calibration, then 10 interleaved in Task 1 and 25 in Task 2 without feedback. The accuracy of the interleaved ones is the measured strength of the high evidence; strength trials carry no rating and no further evidence and are not part of the confidence model.

The simulated observers rated confidence as 1 + 8·σ(L0 + b ± w·e) plus Gaussian noise, rounded to the scale. Group values put in: wd = 0.92 at 90° and 0.47 at 0°; b = 0.42 and wc = 0.88 at both mappings. Participants differed around these values. The random seed was fixed before any result was seen. Files were written in the layout and number format of the online task.

Ratings were analysed with four models fitted per participant and mapping by maximum likelihood with a censored-Gaussian response function: an ideal observer, asymmetric weights, a commitment term, and both. Inference used the parameters of the model with both, fixed in advance. The primary test was the paired comparison of log wd between mappings, referred to the t distribution and to a parametric-bootstrap null in which each participant's session was re-simulated from their fitted parameters with wd equal at both mappings.

Results

Data quality and exclusions

Participants were excluded for overall accuracy outside [.60, .85], accuracy at either mapping outside [.55, .85], one rating on more than 90% of trials, a median rating time below 850 ms, or more than 5% timeouts. 0 of 10 participants met a criterion, leaving N = 10 and 1,800 trials. No model fit failed.

Manipulation checks

The staircase held accuracy at the intended level under both mappings (0°: M = .70, SD = .02; 90°: M = .71, SD = .02), with no difference between them, t(9) = −1.64, p = .136, dz = −0.52 (Figure 1). It reached that level at a higher control proportion under the rotated mapping (M = .52 vs. .43), t(9) = −2.03, p = .073, dz = −0.64, which confirms that the rotation made control harder to detect and that the staircase compensated.

The strong evidence was delivered where the design aims and equally under both mappings (Table 2). Strength-trial accuracy was 86.8% (0°) and 87.0% (90°); the difference was −0.2 percentage points, t(9) = −0.10, p = .920, dz = −0.03. Equivalence within ±5 points: TOST p = .008. The tracked offset moved from its start value of 0.80 to the following means (first → last ten strength trials, 0°: 0.79 → 0.87; 90°: 0.83 → 0.92). The live threshold drifted by +0.08 (0°) and −0.30 (90°) logit over the session; the difference between mappings, the quantity that would bias the primary contrast, was +0.38, t(9) = 2.64, p = .027, dz = 0.84.

The evidence samples differed in strength as intended. As measured, the weak sample corresponded to 70% (0°) and 71% (90°), the strong sample to 87% and 87%. The mean number of incorrect Task 2 trials per participant and mapping, which identify wd, was 17.8 and 17.1.

Metacognitive sensitivity

Metacognitive sensitivity was estimated from Task 1 by maximum likelihood (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012) with ratings in three bins. Pooled over mappings, d′ was 1.05 (SD = 0.21) and meta-d′ 1.21 (SD = 0.41). Meta-d′ did not differ between mappings (0°: M = 0.91; 90°: M = 1.41), t(9) = −1.86, p = .096, dz = −0.59, nor did the ratio meta-d′/d′, t(9) = −1.40, p = .194, dz = −0.44.

Two limits apply. The simulated observers do not generate confidence from a signal-detection model, so there is no true ratio to recover here; this section shows that the analysis runs and finds no difference where none was built in. And with 30 trials per mapping the per-person ratio is very noisy (SD = 0.67 at 0°): for the comparison between mappings the hierarchical estimator used in work package 1 is the appropriate tool.

Confidence after further evidence

Confidence moved in the direction of the evidence (Figure 2). After correct choices it rose with evidence strength and approached the top of the scale under both mappings. After incorrect choices it fell, and fell further under the rotated mapping: following the strong sample, mean confidence in an incorrect choice was 4.60 at 0° and 3.62 at 90°. The descriptive slope of confidence over evidence level on incorrect trials was shallower at 0° (M = 0.57) than at 90° (M = 1.11), t(9) = −3.18, p = .011, dz = −1.00. Because correct trials start near the ceiling of the scale, raw slopes are reported for description only.

Computational model

Model comparison. Summed over participants and mappings, the model with asymmetric weights had the lowest BIC, followed by the model with weights and commitment (ΔBIC = 0.0); the commitment-only model (ΔBIC = 6.8) and the ideal observer (ΔBIC = 253.6) fitted clearly worse (Table 3). BIC therefore preferred the simpler model although the data were generated with a commitment term: at this trial count the penalty for the fourth parameter outweighs a commitment of this size. Inference nevertheless uses the model with both terms, as fixed in advance, because a model without b attributes differences in commitment to wd.

Primary hypothesis: the mapping changes how disconfirming evidence is used. The disconfirmatory weight was lower under the direct mapping (geometric mean wd = 0.43) than under the rotated mapping (0.77), a reduction of 44%. The difference in log wd was −0.58, 95% CI [−1.11, −0.05], t(9) = −2.47, p = .036, dz = −0.78; Wilcoxon signed-rank p = .006 (Table 4, Figure 3A). Referred to the bootstrap null (300 replicates), the observed statistic gave p = .023 (Figure 4). Under that null the paired difference averaged 0.07, so the estimator carries no material bias toward either mapping in this sample.

Commitment. The commitment parameter did not differ between mappings (0°: M = 0.32; 90°: M = 0.36), t(9) = −0.25, p = .806, dz = −0.08 (Figure 3B). The confirmatory weight did not differ either, t(9) = 1.26, p = .238, dz = 0.40. The difference between mappings is therefore specific to the use of disconfirming evidence, as built in.

Is disconfirming evidence under-used at all? Averaged over mappings, log wd was −0.56 (wd = 0.57). An ideal observer passed through the same pipeline does not return wd = 1 but 1.02 (log 0.02; 200 replicates), because the strength of the strong sample is over-estimated. Against this benchmark the observed value gave p = .005 overall; by mapping, 0°: observed 0.43 against a benchmark of 1.03, p = .005; 90°: observed 0.77 against 1.01, p = .129. A test against 1 would have returned t(9) = −3.30, p = .009, d = −1.04, overstating the evidence.

Secondary analysis. Meta-d′ from Task 1 was related to the sensitivity of Task 2 confidence to the further evidence at r = −.03 (n = 10), an estimate too imprecise at this sample size to interpret.

Validation against the known truth

Because the participants were simulated, every estimate can be compared with the value that produced it (Table 5, Figure 5).

The difference between mappings is recovered. The mean difference in log wd put in was −0.67; the analysis returned −0.58. Individual differences were recovered at r = −.19, individual weights at r = .59. The recovered differences were more variable than the true ones (SD = 0.74 vs. 0.23): most of the spread between people in the fitted values is measurement error, as the power analysis had indicated.

The level of the weak sample was estimated at 0.84 (put in 0.97) and of the strong sample at 1.89 (put in 2.17) at 0°, and at 1.90 (put in 1.98) at 90°. The strong sample is not extrapolated from the staircase's range: its accuracy is measured on strength trials, shrunk toward the group value, and the psychometric function only supplies variation around that level.

Consequences for the study

The primary test stays as planned: the paired contrast on log wd from the model with weights and commitment recovers the effect and is not biased toward either mapping.

  1. Strength trials replace the extrapolation. Done: a strength-calibration block (20 choice-only trials per mapping) sets each person's high offset, 10 + 25 interleaved strength trials keep it at about 85% and measure it; the level of the evidence is that measured accuracy.
  2. Report the checks in Table 2. Equal strength-trial accuracy across mappings (TOST), convergence of the offset and the drift of the live threshold are preregistered diagnostics, not assumptions.
  3. Test the level of wd against the ideal-observer benchmark, not against 1. Done: the analysis script produces the benchmark from each participant's own fitted parameters, re-simulating the same two-track design.
  4. Estimate metacognitive efficiency hierarchically. Open: thirty Task 1 trials per mapping do not support a per-person ratio.
  5. Expect BIC to prefer the simpler model, and say so in the preregistration. Open: this is not evidence against a commitment term of the size Rollwage et al. (2018) reported; the model used for inference is fixed in advance for that reason.

Sample size. The effect built in here, a reduction of 49%, is more than twice the smallest effect the full study is powered to detect (20% at N = 150). Ten participants suffice to show that the analysis returns what was put in; they say nothing about whether an effect of realistic size will be found.
