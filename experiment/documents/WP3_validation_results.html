Simulated data. Ten simulated participants built to behave as the hypothesis states. These are not empirical results; the numbers show what the planned analysis returns when the truth is known.

A Second Look · work package 3 · validation of the planned analysis

Does the analysis measure what we set out to measure?

The complete planned analysis, run on data files in the exact layout the online task writes, from simulated participants whose true parameters are known.

Summary

The primary measure works. The simulated participants used disconfirming evidence 49% less under the direct mapping than under the rotated one. The analysis recovered a reduction of 54% (log difference put in −0.67, recovered −0.77). The contrast was significant at N = 10, t(9) = −4.35, p = .002, dz = −1.38, bootstrap p = .003.

The absolute level does not. Fitted wd came out at 0.39 and 0.85 where 0.47 and 0.92 had been put in. The strength of the strong evidence sample is over-estimated, which pulls every weight down by about the same factor. The contrast between mappings is unaffected; a test of wd against 1 is not valid and is replaced by a benchmark below.

Five changes follow for the real study, three of them already made. They are listed in the last section.

Method

Ten participants were simulated. Each judged which of two circles they controlled, under a direct (0°) and a rotated (90°) mapping in counterbalanced order, with control proportion set by a 1-up-2-down staircase that ran throughout. Per mapping there were 30 Task 1 trials (decision, then confidence rating on a 9-point scale) and 60 Task 2 trials (decision, a further 3 s of evidence, then the rating). The further evidence was of the same strength as the decision sample (low) or 1.2 logit stronger (high), and always came from the true target.

The simulated observers rated confidence as 1 + 8·σ(L0 + b ± w·e) plus Gaussian noise, rounded to the scale. Group values put in: wd = 0.92 at 90° and 0.47 at 0°; b = 0.42 and wc = 0.88 at both mappings. Participants differed around these values. The random seed was fixed before any result was seen. Files were written in the layout and number format of the online task.

Ratings were analysed with four models fitted per participant and mapping by maximum likelihood with a censored-Gaussian response function: an ideal observer, asymmetric weights, a commitment term, and both. Inference used the parameters of the model with both, fixed in advance. The primary test was the paired comparison of log wd between mappings, referred to the t distribution and to a parametric-bootstrap null in which each participant's session was re-simulated from their fitted parameters with wd equal at both mappings.

Results

Data quality and exclusions

Participants were excluded for overall accuracy outside [.60, .85], accuracy at either mapping outside [.55, .85], one rating on more than 90% of trials, a median rating time below 850 ms, or more than 5% timeouts. 0 of 10 participants met a criterion, leaving N = 10 and 1,800 trials. No model fit failed.

Manipulation checks

The staircase held accuracy at the intended level under both mappings (0°: M = .71, SD = .02; 90°: M = .70, SD = .02), with no difference between them, t(9) = 1.45, p = .182, dz = 0.46 (Figure 1). It reached that level at a higher control proportion under the rotated mapping (M = .54 vs. .45), t(9) = −4.19, p = .002, dz = −1.33, which confirms that the rotation made control harder to detect and that the staircase compensated.

The evidence samples differed in strength as intended. Expressed as the accuracy a sample would support, the weak sample corresponded to 73% (0°) and 68% (90°), the strong sample to 96% and 93%. The mean number of incorrect Task 2 trials per participant and mapping, which identify wd, was 16.7 and 17.0.

Metacognitive sensitivity

Metacognitive sensitivity was estimated from Task 1 by maximum likelihood (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012) with ratings in three bins. Pooled over mappings, d′ was 1.08 (SD = 0.34) and meta-d′ 1.62 (SD = 0.68). Meta-d′ did not differ between mappings (0°: M = 1.46; 90°: M = 1.58), t(9) = −0.30, p = .774, dz = −0.09, nor did the ratio meta-d′/d′, t(9) = 0.60, p = .566, dz = 0.19.

Two limits apply. The simulated observers do not generate confidence from a signal-detection model, so there is no true ratio to recover here; this section shows that the analysis runs and finds no difference where none was built in. And with 30 trials per mapping the per-person ratio is very noisy (SD = 1.19 at 0°): for the comparison between mappings the hierarchical estimator used in work package 1 is the appropriate tool.

Confidence after further evidence

Confidence moved in the direction of the evidence (Figure 2). After correct choices it rose with evidence strength and approached the top of the scale under both mappings. After incorrect choices it fell, and fell further under the rotated mapping: following the strong sample, mean confidence in an incorrect choice was 3.66 at 0° and 2.63 at 90°. The descriptive slope of confidence over evidence level on incorrect trials was shallower at 0° (M = 0.99) than at 90° (M = 1.54), t(9) = −5.05, p < .001, dz = −1.60. Because correct trials start near the ceiling of the scale, raw slopes are reported for description only.

Computational model

Model comparison. Summed over participants and mappings, the model with asymmetric weights had the lowest BIC, followed by the model with weights and commitment (ΔBIC = 16.7); the commitment-only model (ΔBIC = 51.7) and the ideal observer (ΔBIC = 227.9) fitted clearly worse (Table 2). BIC therefore preferred the simpler model although the data were generated with a commitment term: at this trial count the penalty for the fourth parameter outweighs a commitment of this size. Inference nevertheless uses the model with both terms, as fixed in advance, because a model without b attributes differences in commitment to wd.

Primary hypothesis: the mapping changes how disconfirming evidence is used. The disconfirmatory weight was lower under the direct mapping (geometric mean wd = 0.39) than under the rotated mapping (0.85), a reduction of 54%. The difference in log wd was −0.77, 95% CI [−1.17, −0.37], t(9) = −4.35, p = .002, dz = −1.38; Wilcoxon signed-rank p = .004 (Table 3, Figure 3A). Referred to the bootstrap null (300 replicates), the observed statistic gave p = .003 (Figure 4). Under that null the paired difference averaged 0.05, so the estimator carries no material bias toward either mapping in this sample.

Commitment. The commitment parameter did not differ between mappings (0°: M = 0.30; 90°: M = 0.32), t(9) = −0.29, p = .782, dz = −0.09 (Figure 3B). The confirmatory weight did not differ either, t(9) = 0.24, p = .814, dz = 0.08. The difference between mappings is therefore specific to the use of disconfirming evidence, as built in.

Is disconfirming evidence under-used at all? Averaged over mappings, log wd was −0.55 (wd = 0.58). An ideal observer passed through the same pipeline does not return wd = 1 but 0.83 (log −0.19; 200 replicates), because the strength of the strong sample is over-estimated. Against this benchmark the observed value gave p = .015 overall; by mapping, 0°: observed 0.39 against a benchmark of 0.83, p = .005; 90°: observed 0.85 against 0.82, p = .876. A test against 1 would have returned t(9) = −4.91, p < .001, d = −1.55, overstating the evidence.

Secondary analysis. Meta-d′ from Task 1 was related to the sensitivity of Task 2 confidence to the further evidence at r = .19 (n = 10), an estimate too imprecise at this sample size to interpret.

Validation against the known truth

Because the participants were simulated, every estimate can be compared with the value that produced it (Table 4, Figure 5).

The difference between mappings is recovered. The mean difference in log wd put in was −0.67; the analysis returned −0.77. Individual differences were recovered at r = .74, individual weights at r = .83. The recovered differences were more variable than the true ones (SD = 0.56 vs. 0.23): most of the spread between people in the fitted values is measurement error, as the power analysis had indicated.

The level is not. The weak sample's strength was estimated accurately (1.02 against 1.09 at 0°), the strong sample's was not (3.65 against 3.17 at 0°; 3.31 against 2.81 at 90°). The strong sample lies outside the range of control proportions the staircase visits, so its strength is extrapolated from a psychometric slope that is itself over-estimated (median 2.77 and 2.32 against 2.17). An over-estimated evidence strength is compensated by an under-estimated weight. In a separate check on 120 simulated participants per cell the fitted weights were 0.75 (0°) and 0.77 (90°) of the true values when the calibration trials entered the psychometric fit, as they do here, and 0.67 and 0.66 when they did not. A weaker strong sample (0.8 instead of 1.2 logit) left the factor unchanged. The factor is the same at both mappings, which is why the contrast survives.

Consequences for the study

The primary test stays as planned: the paired contrast on log wd from the model with weights and commitment recovers the effect and is not biased toward either mapping.

  1. Test the level of wd against the ideal-observer benchmark, not against 1. Done: the analysis script produces the benchmark from each participant's own fitted parameters.
  2. Write the calibration trials to the data file. Done: the online task did not log them; it does now. They reduce the bias in the level from about one third to about one quarter.
  3. Write every collected field to the data file. Done: fullscreen exits, the second quiz counter and the Prolific study and session identifiers were collected but never written.
  4. Estimate metacognitive efficiency hierarchically. Open: thirty Task 1 trials per mapping do not support a per-person ratio.
  5. Expect BIC to prefer the simpler model, and say so in the preregistration. Open: this is not evidence against a commitment term of the size Rollwage et al. (2018) reported; the model used for inference is fixed in advance for that reason.

Sample size. The effect built in here, a reduction of 49%, is more than twice the smallest effect the full study is powered to detect (20% at N = 150). Ten participants suffice to show that the analysis returns what was put in; they say nothing about whether an effect of realistic size will be found.
