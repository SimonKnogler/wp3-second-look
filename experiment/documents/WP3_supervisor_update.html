Confidence in Control

Two experiments, one question: is confidence about our own agency a read-out of the evidence — or a belief that expectation can bend and evidence can revise?

Simon KnoglerLMU Munich≈ 9 min readinteractive — move, drag, click

Start here

You have driven for years. You expect the car to answer every input. Then the road ices over. Your actual control drops — but what happens to your confidence that you are in control?

One account says the experienced driver, with a precise internal model, feels the loss sharply: the prediction fails, and confidence collapses with it. Another says the strong expectation makes her overconfident — the belief outlives the control. A learner on the same road, with no model to lean on, watches what the car does and updates as she goes.

That scene is the whole project. A judgment of one's own control draws on a sensorimotor signal — but also on what we expected beforehand and what we learn afterwards. We ask how open such judgments are to that extra information, and whether it depends on where the control signal comes from. And we ask it about confidence specifically, for a reason that will matter later: confidence is the one variable we can hold to a standard.

The lever · two sources of the control signal

Same accuracy, different computation

At 0° the cursor moves as your hand moves. A forward model predicts the outcome, and the match between prediction and feedback is the signal. At 90° the mapping is rotated: the prediction fails, and you find your circle by detecting which one's motion correlates with yours over time.

What we are after

Read-out, or belief?

On the classical view, confidence is a read-out: a function of the evidence at the moment you decide, and nothing else. On the newer expected-precision view it is a belief — it incorporates what you expected, and it can be revised by what arrives afterwards. Three properties separate the two, and our two experiments test one each:

Agency ratings can be shifted. Confidence can be wrong.Sensitivity has a benchmark (meta-d′), bias has one (accuracy), revision has one (Bayes). That is why the paper is about confidence.

Read together: the read-out is equally good in both lanes — people discriminate their own hits from misses just as well at 90°. What differs is what the judgment admits. Under prediction it ignores the prior; under inference it lets the prior in. Malleable where the model is missing, stable where it holds.

Study 2 · after the decision · work package 3 · online, N = 150

Feed in evidence — after they've committed

Now the expectation manipulation is gone. Instead, on half the trials, more evidence arrives after the choice has been made: the same two circles, same sides, same truth — a fresh stretch of movement, as clear as the first or clearer. It always points to the truth, so it confirms the people who were right and contradicts the people who were wrong. Then they rate how likely their first choice was correct.

What the template looks like — and what we predict

In Rollwage's data everyone drops when shown they were wrong; confirming evidence barely moves the (already high) confidence on correct trials. The interesting part is not the drop, it is how much — and whether that depends on where the control signal came from. Drag the weights to see the hypotheses as data patterns.

How we measure it

One line per rating

Every Task-2 rating is fitted as a log-odds line. The baseline comes from Task 1 (separately for hits and misses). Then a commitment term, then the new evidence — converted to a log-likelihood ratio through each person's own psychometric function — weighted by w:

rating = 1 + 8 · σ( L0 + b + wc · e )    on correct trials
rating = 1 + 8 · σ( L0 + b − wd · e )    on incorrect trials
ideal observer: wc = wd = 1, b = 0 · fitted per person × mapping by censored-Gaussian ML · compared by BIC

Why wd and not the raw slopes? Correct trials start near the top of the scale, so their slope is compressed by the ceiling — on simulated data the raw confirm-vs-disconfirm index comes out with the wrong sign. Incorrect trials start mid-scale and have room to move. wd is the well-identified quantity; the 0° vs 90° contrast on it is the primary test.

Can we detect it?

N is capped at 150 — so we asked the question backwards

Instead of "how many people for effect X", we simulated 6,600 participants through the complete pipeline — staircase, psychometric fit, exclusions, the actual fitter — and asked what the smallest detectable effect is at the N we can afford.

Before and after the decision

Both studies use the same instrument and the same two signal sources. What differs is what gets added and when: Study 1 feeds in a learned expectation — never part of the trial's own evidence — before the choice; Study 2 feeds in a second, fresh look at that same evidence, after the choice is already made. The question both ask: is confidence locked in at commitment, or open to revision — the same three properties as the table above.
